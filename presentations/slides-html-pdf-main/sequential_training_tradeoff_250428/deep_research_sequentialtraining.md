Evaluating Sequential Data Scheduling for Compliance-Performance Tradeoffs in a 100B LLM
Introduction
Large Language Models (LLMs) are often trained on massive corpora with varying levels of content filtering. Compliance data refers to training content that strictly adheres to legal, ethical, and copyright guidelines (highly filtered), whereas less compliant data may include potentially controversial or copyrighted content (minimally filtered). Partner A proposes a sequential training scheme for a 100B-parameter LLM to study the compliance-performance tradeoff, under a compute budget of 2.5 trillion tokens. The plan is to train one large model through three stages: starting on a fully compliant dataset (100% filtered), then continuing on a medium-compliance dataset (~50% filtered), and finally on a minimally filtered dataset. Intermediate checkpoints after each stage (Model-HC, Model-MC, Model-LC) would be saved to examine performance changes. However, other partners raise concerns about the scientific validity and feasibility of this approach. Key objections include: potential confounding effects from sequential training (more training time for later stages), data quality differences across compliance levels, and whether 2.5T tokens is sufficient to fully train a 100B model. They propose alternatives, such as training multiple smaller models independently on each compliance level or using a shared base model that diverges into compliance-specific branches. Recent literature on curriculum learning (e.g., scheduling data from clean to noisy) and scaling laws also inform this debate. This report will analyze: (1) the scientific rigor of Partner A’s sequential design, (2) pros/cons of alternative experimental designs (including scaling law considerations), (3) evidence from recent studies on data scheduling vs. uniform sampling (with emphasis on findings from Schaefer et al. 2024 [cite: 2404.07982v4, 13]), (4) differences in data quality across compliance tiers (drawing on Liu et al. 2025 [cite: 2412.09460v4, 7]), (5) whether 2.5T tokens suffice for a 100B model (per Chinchilla scaling and optimizer advances like SCION [cite: 2502.07529v1, 3]), and (6) a synthesis of compromise approaches that balance scientific rigor and engineering constraints. Throughout, we will cite relevant research and include tables to summarize key comparisons.
1. Scientific Rigor of Sequential Compliance Training
Partner A’s design sequentially exposes one large model to datasets of decreasing compliance. The intended analysis is to observe how relaxing data filtering affects performance. However, this approach poses scientific validity challenges:
Confounding by Training Time: In a sequential setup, later stages (with lower compliance data) inherently involve more total training steps. Any performance gains in Model-LC (trained on all 3 stages) over Model-HC (trained only on stage 1 data) could be due simply to extra training time or more tokens consumed, not the data’s compliance level. In other words, compliance level is confounded with training duration/compute. As one partner noted, making causal claims about compliance vs. performance is risky if more compute always improves performance up to saturation. The high-compliance model gets fewer total tokens; the low-compliance model sees 3× more. This makes it difficult to attribute differences specifically to data compliance without controlling for total training tokens.
Mitigation via Controls: To strengthen causal inference, Partner A could incorporate controls such as:
Training a baseline uniform-mix model on all data shuffled together (to match total tokens) and comparing it to the sequential approach. If the sequential model outperforms the uniform model when both see 2.5T tokens, that suggests scheduling had an effect beyond just more data.
Using early stopping points: e.g. train separate 100B models each for 0.83T tokens on HC, MC, LC data respectively (equalizing compute), to isolate data effects. However, training three separate 100B models at 0.83T each may exceed the budget.
An Ablation: After full sequential training, fine-tune a copy of Model-HC on just additional high-compliance tokens (instead of switching to MC data) for the same number of steps as stage 2. This tests if performance jumps are from data change or just continued training.
Validity of Causal Claims: With the current sequential plan, claims like “Using less filtered data improves X% on task Y” would be correlational. It’s scientifically rigorous only if alternative explanations (like total tokens) are ruled out. Without proper controls, conclusions must be tempered (“we observed associations between compliance level and performance, under sequential training conditions”).
Other partners suggest curriculum learning insights: gradually introducing harder or noisier data is reminiscent of curricula, but literature shows mixed results. Recent work by Schäfer et al. (2024) found that for real multilingual data, fancy language scheduling did not uniformly improve performance; uniform sampling often worked best. Their controlled experiments with “cloned” (identical) languages showed imbalanced training can boost the weaker language, but in real languages, imbalanced schedules had inconsistent benefits. In our case, compliant vs. non-compliant data are not identical distributions, so curriculum effects are uncertain. It’s plausible that starting on “easy” (clean) data then adding “hard” (messy) data could help or hurt, and results must be interpreted carefully. Summary: Scientifically, Partner A’s design provides one data point (one large model’s trajectory). It may be hard to disentangle why performance changes. To improve rigor, incorporate parallel controls or ablations, and be cautious in attributing causality to data compliance alone.
2. Alternative Experimental Designs
Several alternative designs have been proposed to investigate compliance-performance tradeoffs more rigorously:
A. Independent Small Models (e.g., 3B) per Compliance Level: Instead of one 100B model, train three separate smaller models (say 3B parameters each) from scratch on High-Compliance, Medium-Compliance, and Low-Compliance data (each with a portion of the 2.5T tokens, or possibly less since these are smaller models). This gives direct comparisons of how data compliance affects performance without sequential confounding. The smaller size is suggested to save compute, but we must consider scaling laws when extrapolating results to 100B.
Strengths: Clear isolation of data effect, easier to run multiple trials. Past studies (e.g., Liu et al. 2025) trained 17 models of 7B on different curated corpora to compare their performance. That approach yields scientifically cleaner results about data influence.
Weaknesses: Scaling differences – a 3B model’s behavior might not predict a 100B’s behavior. Some effects (like being undertrained or overfitting to certain data peculiarities) could differ at scale. Also, training three separate models might in total equal or exceed the cost of one 100B sequentially (depending on token allocations and parallelization).
B. Shared Foundation + Divergence: Train a single base model on the full 100% compliant data for some portion of tokens, then fork it into three copies. Continue training one copy solely on HC data (control), the second on MC data, and the third on LC data for the remaining tokens. This way, all models start from the same foundation, then experience different data. For instance:
Stage 1: Train 100B on 0.5T tokens of HC data (foundation).
Stage 2: Copy the model into three; train each on an additional 2.0T tokens of HC, MC, and LC respectively.
This yields Model-HC-full, Model-MC (mixed), Model-LC (low filter), each seeing a total of 2.5T tokens but with different data compositions (and identical initial weights).
Strengths: Controls for initial conditions and model architecture. Differences at the end can be more confidently attributed to data differences since total compute (2.5T each) is equalized. This design aligns with concerns about confounding and is akin to branching experiments in curriculum learning research.
Weaknesses: We end up training 3 × 2.5T = 7.5T tokens – triple the budget (unless the 2.0T in stage 2 is split among the three, e.g., 0.67T each, but then each model only sees 1.17T total which might under-train a 100B model). Compute cost is a major drawback. If we truly have only 2.5T total, we might allocate ~0.83T to each branch, which likely under-fits a 100B model significantly (per scaling laws discussed later).
C. Uniform Mixture Baseline: As a simpler alternative, train the 100B model on a uniform mixture of all data (no sequential curriculum) for 2.5T tokens. This provides a baseline for overall performance if compliance levels are mixed. Many large models (GPT-3, LLaMA, etc.) use a blended dataset with some filtering but not a strict curriculum from clean to dirty. If the sequential approach yields a better model than the uniformly trained one, that’s evidence in favor of scheduling. If it doesn’t, then scheduling may be unnecessary or even detrimental, as some studies on curriculum vs. mixed sampling suggest.
D. Partial Curriculum with Resets: A twist on Partner A’s plan to address confounding could be resetting or reinitializing some aspects when changing data. For example, when switching from HC to MC data, one might reset optimizer state or use a fresh learning rate schedule to mitigate simply “coasting” on momentum from earlier training. This was done in some multilingual fine-tuning experiments to avoid catastrophic forgetting or to fairly allocate learning capacity (e.g., resetting Adam moments when switching tasks). It is not a full alternative design, but rather a technique to make sequential training more scientifically interpretable.
Scaling Law Considerations:
We know from the Chinchilla scaling law that for a given compute budget, there’s an optimal model size vs. data size tradeoff. Chinchilla suggests roughly model and data scaling in tandem: e.g., a 70B model was paired with ~1.4T tokens in the original Chinchilla analysis. For a 100B model, the “Chinchilla-optimal” data might be around ~2.0T tokens (this is a rough extrapolation). So 2.5T tokens for 100B is slightly above that, which could be beneficial, as more data should improve performance until diminishing returns kick in. However, significantly underfeeding a model (too few tokens for its size) leads to under-training – a core worry if we split data among multiple models. A smaller 3B model is much easier to saturate with far fewer tokens. Hence, alternative A (three 3B models) might all reach near-optimal training on a subset of 2.5T tokens each (even, say, 0.3T might suffice for 3B per Chinchilla scaling), whereas a 100B model ideally should consume trillions. Thus, alternative designs must be weighed not only for scientific rigor but also in terms of learning dynamics at scale. This is a big reason Partner A’s single-run plan is attractive from an engineering view: it maximizes final model quality by using all 2.5T on one set of weights. Summary: Alternatives offer cleaner comparisons but face trade-offs in compute and applicability to a full-scale model. A compromise might be to use a medium-scale model (e.g., 10B or 20B) for comparison experiments, or to run a smaller pilot study to validate the concept, before committing 100B resources.
3. Curriculum Learning & Data Scheduling Evidence
Curriculum learning – feeding data in a structured order rather than uniformly at random – has a mixed track record in LLM training. The sequential compliance idea is essentially a curriculum from “easy” (compliant) to “hard” (unfiltered). What does recent research say? A relevant study is Schaefer et al. (2024) on multilingual training [cite: 2404.07982v4]. Key insights include:
Cloned Language Setup: In a perfectly controlled scenario (cloned languages, where two datasets are identical in content but treated as different “languages”), they found that an imbalanced schedule (training more on one language than the other) actually improved performance on the under-represented language and overall alignment. This suggests that starting with one distribution and then adding another later can help, at least when the distributions are fundamentally the same kind of data.
Real Languages: However, when applying scheduling to actual different languages (like English and French), the gains were not consistent. In fact, a simple uniform mix often outperformed scheduled approaches that overweight one language early or late. Longer training with a bad schedule even widened the performance gap against the uniform baseline. The authors note that none of their tested multi-stage schedules beat the uniform mixture on both languages simultaneously (you could trade off one for the other).
Takeaway: “For real languages, we do not find improvements on all languages due to the tested language schedules. However, they allow for trading off performance in different languages.” [cite: 2404.07982v4, 13]. In other words, curricula can shift where the model does better, but there was no one schedule that was strictly superior in all aspects. They speculate that better schedules or coordination with learning rate might help, but it’s non-trivial.
Applying this to compliance levels: If “High Compliance vs. Low Compliance” is analogous to two domains, a uniform mix might yield a more balanced model (good compliance and performance). A sequential schedule could tilt the model to perform better on one end at the expense of the other. For instance, starting on only compliant data might imbue the model with safe, in-distribution behavior early, and then adding low-compliance data could boost raw knowledge or capability. But it might also erode some safety or consistency. Conversely, starting with unfiltered data first (not proposed, but hypothetically) then fine-tuning on compliance might yield a powerful but safer model. The order could matter. The literature suggests caution: a sequential strategy might not strictly dominate a well-shuffled training. It may yield trade-offs – which could be acceptable if that’s the research goal (to explore compliance vs performance trade-off explicitly). If the aim is purely best final performance, a uniform mix or a very carefully tuned curriculum might be better. One more point: Curriculum vs. Anti-Curriculum. Some evidence shows that training on harder tasks first then easier tasks (anti-curriculum) can sometimes work better in ML, because the model doesn’t get complacent. In compliance terms, “harder” could mean the noisy, less filtered data. But this is speculative; the safe approach is often uniform sampling with appropriate mixing ratios. Conclusion for Section 3: The study [2404.07982v4] indicates uniform training is robust, and multi-stage scheduling can backfire if not done well. Partner A’s sequential plan should be benchmarked against a uniform baseline. If sequential underperforms, that suggests an inherent inefficiency in the curriculum approach.
4. Data Quality Across Compliance Levels (Evidence from Literature)
The compliance levels likely correlate with data quality differences: highly compliant data may be over-filtered, mostly from high-quality sources but possibly domain-limited, whereas low-compliance data includes more diverse raw web text (including copyrighted or toxic content but also technical, niche knowledge). It’s critical to examine what we expect at each tier: A recent paper by Liu et al. (2025) [cite: 2412.09460v4] studied the impact of including or excluding copyrighted (hence non-compliant) materials in training a Norwegian LLM (7B size). They created multiple datasets:
Publisher-controlled high-quality data (copyrighted books & news) – analogous to “low compliance” in a legal sense (since it includes copyrighted text without license, except they had special access).
Public domain / web data – more compliant and common.
Filtered subsets like fiction vs non-fiction.
Key findings relevant to compliance vs. performance:
Adding high-quality non-compliant data (e.g., copyrighted books & newspapers) significantly improved model performance on a range of tasks. The improvements were especially notable in specialized tasks (the model got better at Norwegian language tasks when it had more diverse, rich text). This underscores that from a performance standpoint, the least compliant data (in terms of licensing) was the most valuable (because it was high-quality text). The model with an “extended” dataset including these materials had the highest skill score (average +6.7% improvement over baseline).
Conversely, data that was perhaps too compliant or curated, like exclusively fiction books which might have been available or public, hurt performance slightly (–1.4% drop). They found fiction data made the model more diverse in text generation but worse on factual or task-oriented evaluations. This suggests that “compliant” doesn’t always equal “useful”; one partner’s warning that highly filtered data might have low information value or be biased seems valid. If the HC dataset is, say, mostly Wikipedia and carefully screened content, it might not teach the 100B model as much as the raw mix would.
Data Representativeness: The same paper highlighted that the advantage of the high-quality (non-compliant) data was less pronounced if the model was warm-started from a larger English model. This indicates that a lot of value in additional data can be redundant if the model already saw similar text in pretraining. In Partner A’s case, we are training from scratch, so each new data source could bring new knowledge—unless the compliant data and non-compliant data overlap in content domains.
Bias and Safety: While not deeply discussed in the PDF, it’s understood that highly compliant data might be biased (e.g., overly polite, Western-centric if from certain sources) and low compliance data might introduce toxicity or unwanted behaviors. Partner A’s experiment inherently would let us see if the model gets “edgier” or less safe when fed the LC data, versus HC. This is a valid outcome to measure (compliance trade-off), but from a purely performance view, LC likely boosts perplexity-based metrics at some cost to alignment.
Importantly, Liu et al. (2025) also echo that the use of such data raises ethical/legal issues. They argue for compensation and policy to address this. So in a compliance context: even if low-compliance data yields better performance, one might be wary of endorsing it without mitigation (like the need for fine-tuning on instructions or safety to counteract any harmful content learned). From a scientific perspective, an issue is if these datasets differ in many ways (length distributions, domains, languages, etc.), then sequentially training on them means the model is not just seeing “less filtering”; it could be seeing whole new domains. That could confound analysis: is it the compliance (legal/ethical filtering) or just the presence of new domain data that caused changes? If HC data was, say, mostly English Wikipedia, and LC data adds Reddit and non-English content, the model’s improvement may come from multilingual exposure or informal language, not the compliance aspect per se. To address this, one would want to characterize each dataset (e.g., compute perplexity, n-gram overlap with eval tasks, etc.). [cite: 2412.09460v4] essentially did that by splitting fiction vs. non-fiction, etc., to pinpoint what kinds of content yield what effects. In conclusion, the literature and reasoning suggest: Lower compliance (more inclusive) data tends to yield better raw performance. High compliance data alone might lead to a model that is safe but underperforms. The trade-off is real: it’s precisely what the partners want to measure. The key is to ensure differences observed can be attributed to the data differences (thus needing careful experimental design).
5. Compute Budget: Is 2.5T Tokens Enough for a 100B Model?
The compute budget allows 2.5 trillion tokens to train a 100B parameter model. We consider two aspects: (a) adequacy of 2.5T for near-optimal performance, and (b) any insights from optimizer research (like SCION or hyperparameter transfer) to make the most of this budget.
Chinchilla Scaling Law: This empirical law suggests the ideal number of training tokens (N) scales roughly linearly with model parameters (P) for a given compute budget, with an optimal ratio around 20 tokens per parameter (as a ballpark). For 100B parameters, that would be ~2.0 trillion tokens (since Chinchilla’s 70B model used ~1.4T). So 2.5T is slightly above that heuristic, which is promising. If indeed 2.5T is provided, the model should not be undertrained; it might even be a bit over the optimal, which could slightly waste compute but also could squeeze a bit more performance out at convergence.
It’s worth noting that these laws assume high-quality data and optimal training efficiency. If part of the data is less useful (like padded with low info content to meet compliance), the effective tokens might be less. But assuming all tokens are somewhat useful, 2.5T for 100B is decent.
In practice, the only bigger models (GPT-3 was 175B on 0.3T tokens – severely undertrained by Chinchilla standards; PaLM 540B on 2T tokens, also undertrained by those standards; LLaMA 2 70B on 2T tokens, a bit under as well). Many LLMs we know were undertrained due to compute limits. So a 100B with 2.5T might actually achieve better loss than some current models of similar size (assuming the architecture is well-chosen and training is efficient).
Training Efficiency (Optimizers and Hyperparameters): With such a large training run, one crucial aspect is getting the hyperparameters (learning rate schedule, batch size, optimizer type) right. Traditional Adam might require tuning and warmup etc. Recent research (e.g., SCION – Stochastic Conditional Optimization with Norms, [cite: 2502.07529v1]) suggests new optimizers that can transfer hyperparameters from smaller runs and remain stable at large scale:
SCION is notable for “zero-shot hyperparameter transferability,” meaning you could tune on a small model or subset and apply the same settings to the 100B model without retrial. It also reportedly handles large batch sizes better and may not need a warmup​

. If reliable, this could save a lot of trial-and-error in our 100B training.
SCION works by constraining an optimizer on an operator norm level, controlling weights’ growth. It unifies several algorithms and even outperforms Adam in some large-scale tests (they tried up to a 3B model and showed better validation loss and batch robustness).
If we are worried about 2.5T being wasted due to suboptimal learning rate schedules, using SCION or similar could maximize learning. For example, SCION’s invariance to width implies a stepsize that works for 3B might also work for 100B.
Additionally, recent scaling law papers emphasize careful loss curve extrapolation. If by, say, 2.0T tokens the loss is still trending down significantly, maybe the model could use more data. But if it plateaus at 2.0T, then 2.5T is enough or excess. So monitoring validation loss and perhaps employing early stopping or continuing if needed is wise.
Undertraining Risks: If Partner A’s sequential plan is used, one worry is the early stage might undertrain on the HC data (since they might not use all 2.5T on it). For example, if stage 1 uses 0.5T on HC, the model at that point (Model-HC) is extremely undertrained for 100B. So any evaluation of Model-HC vs Model-LC isn’t just data differences but also training stage differences. This ties back to Section 1 on confounding. However, by the end of stage 3, the full 2.5T is used, so Model-LC could be near-fully trained. The challenge: Model-HC is a suboptimal model (due to fewer tokens). Partners need to be careful in interpreting its performance because some might say “of course it’s worse, it only saw 20% of the data amount.” If we had infinite compute, we’d train each stage to convergence (which is alternative B earlier).
Compute Efficiency: Another angle is whether sequential training wastes compute. If the model has to “relearn” some things at each stage that could have been learned earlier with a mix, maybe it’s less efficient. One partner noted that if the high-compliance data is low-info, you might spend a lot of tokens on it for little gain, whereas mixing could have ensured each token is doing work. This is somewhat speculative, but for instance, if HC data is repetitive or lacks diversity, the model might saturate it quickly and then effectively have unused capacity until new data comes in. There is an argument for front-loading the most valuable data early so the model builds general capabilities. If HC is too narrow, that might not happen. Some curriculum strategies suggest interleaving data rather than strict blocks can yield better overall training.
In summary for 2.5T tokens: It appears sufficient for a 100B model to reach good performance (possibly near state-of-the-art for its size), according to scaling laws. The main risk is not the token count, but how those tokens are utilized (order, optimization). The team should leverage modern optimizer research (like SCION’s findings on hyperparameter transfer) [cite: 2502.07529v1, 2] to avoid common pitfalls. Also, keep an eye on the effective batch size and training parallelism—2.5T tokens with a large batch (for efficiency) might need fewer optimization steps versus many small batch updates; the balance can affect model quality (too large batches can harm generalization if not done carefully, though SCION claims to mitigate that).
6. Synthesis & Recommended Compromise Approaches
Balancing scientific rigor with engineering practicality, here are some compromise strategies for Partner A and the team: (i) Use a Smaller-Scale Pilot to Validate Design:
Before spending the 2.5T on the 100B model, perform a pilot experiment on a smaller model (e.g., 10B or even 3B) with the sequential vs. alternative setups. For example, train a 3B model sequentially on HC→MC→LC (with proportionally fewer total tokens), and also train separate 3B models on each dataset independently to convergence. Compare outcomes:
If the sequential 3B’s performance roughly matches a mix of what the independent ones achieve, it suggests the curriculum didn’t harm or overly help – so the design might be acceptable.
If the sequential 3B is far worse than a 3B trained on all data mixed (or far better in some aspects), we learn something without risking the full run. This pilot would cost maybe a few billion tokens – a fraction of 2.5T.
(ii) Incorporate Uniform Data Mixing in the 100B Run:
As a middle ground, do staged training but overlap the stages. For example:
First 0.5T tokens: 100% HC (as planned).
Next 1.0T tokens: Mix HC and MC (instead of pure MC). For instance, gradually increase the fraction of MC over those tokens (a linear annealing from 0 to 100% MC).
Last 1.0T tokens: Mix MC and LC, again maybe gradually shifting to more LC.
This way, there’s no sharp boundary where suddenly completely new data arrives; it’s a smoother curriculum. It might alleviate catastrophic forgetting of early data and still allow observation of how more low-compliance content influences the model. It also reduces the “hard reset” confound of stage-wise training because the model continuously sees a blend. We could still save checkpoints at the 1.5T mark (when LC just starts dominating) etc., though analysis would be more complex. This approach aligns with scheduled mixing strategies sometimes used in curricula, ensuring the model doesn’t overfit to one extreme before seeing the rest. (iii) Reset Optimizer at Stage Transitions:
If sequential stages are used, consider resetting the optimizer state or using a fresh learning rate schedule when introducing MC and LC data. This makes each stage more like a new training phase and can be seen as akin to fine-tuning. It could mitigate the momentum from earlier training dominating the results. However, one has to be careful: resetting too much might lose the benefit of having already learned generic features. Perhaps just a LR warmup could be reintroduced at stages. (iv) Leverage Advanced Optimizers (SCION or others):
Adopt SCION [cite: 2502.07529v1, 1] or similar techniques to ensure stable training across the whole 2.5T. This will make sure the model doesn’t diverge when distribution shifts (some optimizers might spike loss when new data distribution comes in). SCION’s hyperparameter transfer means we can tune on a small subset (like the pilot) and reuse for 100B, ensuring we hit good learning rates from the start. Also, SCION’s robustness to large batch means we can train faster without loss degradation. (v) Evaluate Continuously and Early-Stop if Needed:
During training, evaluate the model (on various benchmarks and safety metrics) at each checkpoint (HC, MC, LC). If, for example, we see that performance plateaus early in stage 3, maybe we don’t need to use all tokens on LC data and could redirect to something else (like more MC or a balanced mix). Or if we see compliance metrics drop sharply at stage 3, we could decide to do a short safety finetune at the end using a small portion of tokens (like RLHF or rule-based filtering) to pull the model back to an acceptable alignment. This wasn’t explicitly asked, but it’s a pragmatic step to ensure the final model isn’t just a raw LC-trained one with all the issues that entails. (vi) Documentation and Transparency:
From a scientific standpoint, carefully document what data goes into each stage (size, domains, average quality metrics). This relates to [cite: 2412.09460v4] which emphasized needing traceable data composition to interpret results. If results need to inform policy or best practices, knowing exactly what “compliance” meant in terms of data content is crucial. (vii) Potential Hybrid: Medium Model + Full Model:
If budget and time allow, consider training a medium-size model (e.g., 10B-20B) in the branching design (Alternative B) fully (with something like 3× tokens). It will be cheaper than 100B and can more thoroughly explore the space of “same model, different data.” Use that insight to guide how to efficiently use the 100B run. For instance, if the 20B experiment shows that LC data yields +5% performance but introduces toxic responses, whereas MC data yields +3% performance with much less toxicity, one might decide to not go all the way to LC in the 100B or to incorporate a safety refinement after LC. Conclusion:
Partner A’s idea targets a very relevant question: How do we quantify the trade-off between compliant training data and model performance? The above analysis indicates it’s a feasible but non-trivial endeavor. Scientifically, the sequential approach must be carefully interpreted due to confounds, but there are ways to mitigate them (controls, baselines, mixing). Practically, training a 100B on 2.5T tokens is right at the frontier of what’s doable; using cutting-edge optimizers and scaling insights is necessary to maximize the outcome. The compromise approach likely involves a combination of:
Running the main 100B experiment (to satisfy engineering goals of producing a strong model)
But designing it in a way that we can extract scientific insights (with baselines or mid-run adjustments). This might mean slight deviations from the pure “one stage then next” plan – perhaps overlapping stages or including an additional baseline model training in parallel (even a smaller one).
By implementing these strategies, the team can ensure that we get a high-performing model and credible evidence on the compliance-performance relationship. The final deliverable could be both a powerful 100B model and a research report (perhaps even publishable) on how data compliance levels affect large-scale LLM training, which would be a valuable contribution to the community.
